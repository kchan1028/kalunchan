Audit date: October 18, 2026. Scope: the current repository, including uncommitted editorial changes and the newly added AOL-to-AI article. This is not a fresh crawl of the deployed website.

The follow-up editorial pass addressed all 89 recorded findings: 88 were resolved through edits, removal or recasting unverified engagement details as illustrative designs. F067 was retained after contextual review because the bold stage names help readers scan six distinct review responsibilities.

The inventory below preserves the original audit and its excerpts. Its source line numbers refer to that earlier snapshot; they are not current edit locations. The [findings CSV](AI-STYLE-FINDINGS.csv) now records each item’s resolution and original status.

## Original findings

89 findings across 46 source files: 69 recommended edits, 14 passages to review in context, and 6 authenticity checks. There are 83 style findings. IDs count passages or clusters, not every repeated word or every rendered occurrence. A shared component may appear on many routes.

These are editorial observations against KC’s requested voice. They do not prove that a passage was generated by AI. A short sentence, a contrast, three items, or a useful bold label can be a deliberate and effective choice. The stronger signals here are accumulation, repeated framing across articles, and language that adds emphasis without adding information.

The main remaining issue is structure rather than punctuation. Familiar contrasts and maxims recur in article bodies even where their closing sections have been edited. The Mentorship page combines fragments, parallel openings, motivational headings and repeated closing lessons. Article summaries and shared copy sometimes retain language removed from the article itself.

## Scope and method

- Inventoried 105 source files: all 61 article files, page components, content data, shared components and site metadata. One article, `learning-hermes-building-ai-automation-around-the-clock`, is development-only.
- Used source-wide searches for contrasts, staged openers, exaggerated claims, question clusters, repeated phrases, short paragraphs and bold-led lists. Read flagged passages in context, all article closing sections, and the relevant page and shared-copy sections.
- Compared recurring language across articles and their listing/summary copy. Consulted `CONTENT-REVIEW.md` for known drafted claims.
- Checked the existing 81-page prerendered output with `BUILD_DIR=.validate npm run audit:writing`: zero failures and zero warnings. There were no source-content edits in this audit, so a new build was not required.
- Inventories and searches provide broad coverage; they are not a line-by-line factual verification or a guarantee that every possible style issue has been found. A zero in the coverage inventory means no finding was recorded, not that the file is certified clean.

The detailed [findings CSV](AI-STYLE-FINDINGS.csv) includes every recorded item with a source line, excerpt, explanation and suggested action. The [coverage CSV](AI-STYLE-COVERAGE.csv) includes all inventoried files.

## Priority

P1 means address first because the passage combines several unwanted habits, is widely reused, overstates an engineering point, or carries a known authenticity concern. P2 means an ordinary editorial improvement. P3 means a lighter or contextual review. Priority describes the value of editing, not the likelihood of AI authorship.

31 P1, 47 P2 and 11 P3 findings. All six authenticity checks are P1; they are excluded from the 83 style findings.

Recommended order:

1. Resolve known drafted first-person claims before polishing them. The public-sector, document-processing and event-driven case studies need source confirmation. Check articles that reuse those case details too.
2. Edit the Mentorship page and shared footer as complete passages. They repeat one leadership message through several dramatic structures.
3. Rework the recurring cheaper-code/judgment contrast and the model-versus-plumbing framing in article bodies.
4. Trim repeated closing lessons, costume/rent metaphors and stock question introductions.
5. Reconcile teasers, summaries, captions and FAQs with the edited article bodies. Preserve SEO intent and keep visible and structured answers consistent.
6. Review bold-led lists and question clusters in their rendered layout. Some should remain.

## Patterns checked without treating them as automatic defects

- **Em/en dash separators:** no remaining article-body occurrences found in the current source check. Brand separators in SEO titles and literal technical content are separate from body prose.
- **Stock vocabulary:** words such as “journey,” “framework,” “robust” or “unlock” were not treated as proof. “Journey” may describe a user flow; “unlock deals” appears in an explicitly hypothetical sales request. No indiscriminate vocabulary ban is recommended.
- **Technical contrasts:** direct calls versus event streams, prototype versus production, authorization versus authentication and deterministic filtering versus inference can convey essential distinctions. Keep the distinctions; edit a staged rhetorical form only when it adds no information.
- **Questions:** the hypothetical requirements example, candidate questions and accessibility checks can be useful. Findings marked Review concern the density or presentation, not the validity of asking them.
- **Bold:** provider names in the privacy policy are useful navigation. Metrics and named review stages may also justify emphasis. A list with bold labels is not automatically slop.
- **Personal details:** the first-race mistakes, collarbone recovery, first-server aside, BBS/IRC preferences and specific hardware/platform references are the material that makes the writing distinctive. Do not smooth those away just because they are casual or uneven.
- **Instructions and examples:** code blocks, commands, literal output and agent prompts are not normal article narration. Do not sanitize their punctuation or rewrite their content as an editorial side effect.
- **FAQs:** short answers may legitimately repeat the article’s point for a different reading path. Check whether each adds a useful answer rather than deleting all repetition by rule.

## Two forms of repetition to distinguish

The site legitimately has recurring convictions: small changes, operating cost, clear ownership, fundamentals and mentoring. Repeating a conviction across relevant articles is reasonable. Repeating the same rhetorical packaging makes those articles feel interchangeable. Keep the convictions and let the engineering situation determine the explanation.

The newer AOL-to-AI article and the DOS-to-AI article also have overlapping closing observations about BBSs, later voice platforms and an incomplete view of AI. Both passages are individually natural. During a later editing pass, decide whether their closeness warrants giving each article a different final observation; it is not included as a definite defect above.

## Limits of the existing automated audit

The audit currently catches em dashes and separator dashes, a narrow “isn’t X. It’s Y” construction, and “Here’s the thing/how” run-ups. It also warns on page-wide bold-label ratios and comma-heavy closing principles. A clean result does not mean the prose meets the full editorial standard.

Specific gaps demonstrated here:

- “The typing got cheaper. The judgment did not” does not match the existing contrast expression.
- Repeated slogans and metaphors across files are not compared.
- The repeated closings, standalone explanations and dramatic fragments are not evaluated semantically.
- A fully bold-led list can escape a page-wide ratio threshold if the same page contains many plain list items.
- Text inside `header` and `footer` is skipped, so hero copy and shared footer language are not reviewed by that parser.
- Structured-data scripts are rightly excluded, but the copy feeding metadata and summaries still needs a separate editorial check.
- Development-only content is absent from production prerendering.
- No punctuation/phrase check establishes whether a first-person claim is supported.

Any future automation should report broad patterns as review candidates, with code and technical examples excluded. It should not automatically delete short paragraphs, questions, bold labels, ordinary contrasts or three-item lists.
